The cascade

Thirteen basins, one long descent.

The park's defining feature is a 300-foot cascade that links its two levels. Water gathers in twin fountains on the upper terrace, then works its way down through thirteen graduated basins — semicircular, and cast largely in concrete — before settling into the reflecting pool at the bottom of the hill.

It's a design borrowed from the water gardens of sixteenth-century Italian villas, built at a scale meant to be felt as much as seen: the sound of the cascade follows you the whole way down the stairs beside it.

Park layout

Two gardens, one hill.

Upper level

The mall

An open lawn flanked by trees, laid out in a formal French style along a single north–south axis. It's where the park breathes: wide grass, long sightlines, and a terrace at the crest with a view south over the cascade and toward downtown.

Lower level

The plaza

The steep southern slope, rendered almost entirely in concrete in an Italianate style inspired by the Villa d'Este. This is the cascade's territory — basins, retaining walls, and stairways down to the reflecting pool and plaza at street level.

On the grounds

A few things to look for.

Joan of Arc

An equestrian statue near the park's northern edge — the only such statue of a woman in Washington, D.C.

Dante

A seated bronze of the poet, tucked along the upper mall's edge, a gift from the city's Italian-American community.

James Buchanan Memorial

A formal memorial terrace on the park's east side, honoring the 15th U.S. president.

Every Sunday, since the 1960s

The drum circle, and the other name.

By the 1960s, the park had become a gathering place for Black activism in the city, and the community's own name for it — Malcolm X Park — took hold even though a 1970 push to make it official in Congress didn't pass. The National Park Service still lists it as Meridian Hill Park; almost everyone who lives nearby calls it the other name.

That history is still active. On Sunday afternoons, a drum circle gathers on the lower plaza — one of the longest-running in the country, and the best-known single reason people make the trip.

Planning a visit

Getting there.

Address

16th Street NW, between W Street and Euclid Street NW, Washington, DC — straddling Columbia Heights and Adams Morgan.

Hours

Open daily, 6 a.m. until dark. The cascade and grounds are free to visit; no reservation needed.

Getting there

Closest Metro stops are Columbia Heights and U Street–Cardozo (Green/Yellow lines), each about a 10-minute walk.
